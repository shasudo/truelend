Every borrower with a two-year-old home loan has had the thought: _rates have moved, mine
hasn't — should I switch?_ The answer is arithmetic, not loyalty. But the arithmetic has more
terms in it than the ads suggest.

## The real cost of switching

A balance transfer isn't free. Budget for:

- **Processing fee** at the new lender — typically 0.25–0.50% of the outstanding, sometimes
  capped, sometimes waived in campaigns.
- **Legal and valuation charges** — ₹5,000–15,000, because the new lender re-verifies the
  property from scratch.
- **Stamp duty on the new mortgage deed** in some states — small percentages on large numbers
  are still real money.
- **Your time** — a transfer is a fresh underwriting: documents, signatures, and four to six
  weeks of follow-up.

## The break-even test

The only question that matters: **do the savings outrun the costs before you exit the loan?**

Take a ₹40 lakh outstanding with 15 years left, moving from 9.4% to 8.7%. The EMI drops by
about ₹1,600 a month. If switching costs ₹40,000 all-in, you break even in roughly two years —
and bank almost ₹2.5 lakh over the remaining tenure if you stay the course.

Now the same move with only five years left: the EMI saving is barely ₹1,300, break-even
stretches past two and a half years, and the total gain may not justify six weeks of paperwork.
**The later you are in the loan, the less a rate cut is worth** — most of the interest has
already been paid.

## The variable everyone forgets

A transfer resets your relationship with prepayment. The cheapest loan is rarely the one with
the lowest sticker rate — it's the one that lets you _finish fastest_. When comparing lenders,
weigh:

- Zero foreclosure charges (standard on floating-rate home loans, but verify for top-ups)
- Whether part-prepayments reduce tenure by default (they should) or just the EMI
- How the top-up, if you take one, is priced — top-ups at "home loan plus 1.5%" quietly undo
  the savings of the transfer

A useful trick: keep paying your _old_ EMI amount after switching. The difference becomes an
automatic monthly prepayment, and the tenure collapses years ahead of schedule.

## When we'd say don't switch

We regularly tell borrowers to stay put: when the rate gap is under ~0.35%, when fewer than
five years remain, when a credit blip would make fresh underwriting risky, or when the current
lender will simply reprice the loan for a small conversion fee — always ask them first; the
threat of a transfer is often as effective as the transfer.
